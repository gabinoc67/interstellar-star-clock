Equations Used to Address the Two Research Problems

1. Required Effective Rate
v_eff = D / t_target

v_eff = 2,537,000 ly / 1 yr = 2,537,000c
2. Required Geometry Ratio
Q = v_eff / v_local

Q = 2,537,000 / 0.90 ≈ 2,818,888.889
3. Equivalent Path Fraction
f_path = v_local / v_eff

f_path = 0.90 / 2,537,000
This is a kinematic equivalent-path requirement, not proof that spacetime can physically create such a path.
4. Trial 3+1D Metric
gμν(θ,x,y,z)
θ represents the front/rear curvature parameters. The inverse problem is solved within this selected metric family.
5. Inverse Target Solver
θ* = arg minθ [ (t_metric(θ) - t_target) / t_target ]²
This replaces the incorrect idea that the transit time alone uniquely determines every possible metric.
6. Einstein Field Equation
Gμν = (8πG/c⁴)Tμν
7. Inverse Einstein Source
Tμν = (c⁴/8πG)Gμν
8. Trial Curvature Energy Requirement
E_curvature* = ∫V |T_00| dV
This simulator uses a finite controlled volume and a magnitude-based bookkeeping estimate. It is a trial-model energy diagnostic, not a new established law of general relativity.
9. Continuous Curvature Power
P_curvature* = E_curvature* / τ_refresh
10. Useful Nuclear Power
P_useful = η_conversion P_reactor
11. Power Feasibility Ratio
R_P = P_useful / P_curvature*

R_P ≥ 1 → trial-model energy budget satisfied
R_P < 1 → trial-model energy budget not satisfied
Passing this equation would mean only that enough power exists under the assumptions of this model. It would not prove that nuclear power actually couples into the required spacetime curvature.
12. Missing Experimental Coupling Law
hμν = F[P_control, geometry, apparatus, time]
This is the physical relationship that would have to be measured experimentally. General relativity relates spacetime geometry to stress-energy, but it does not provide an established engineering equation saying that electrical output from a fission reactor can be converted directly into an arbitrarily selected curvature field.
Important research distinction: The first problem can be converted from a simple "NO" into an inverse mathematical problem. Once a specific family of metrics is selected, the simulator can solve for the member of that family that best satisfies the one-year target. The second problem has two separate parts. The Einstein equations allow the simulator to calculate the source requirement associated with the assumed geometry and compare an estimated energy requirement against continuous reactor output. However, the existence of sufficient reactor power does not establish a physical mechanism for transforming fission-generated energy into the selected spacetime geometry. That final coupling relationship must ultimately be experimentally measured or derived from additional physical theory.
